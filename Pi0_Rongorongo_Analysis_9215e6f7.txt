INTRODUCTION TO RONGORONGO
-------------------------
Rongorongo is one of the world's most enigmatic writing systems, discovered on Easter Island (Rapa Nui) in the 19th century. The script consists of approximately 120 distinct glyphs carved on wooden tablets and other artifacts. Despite numerous attempts by linguists, cryptographers, and archaeologists over the past 150 years, Rongorongo remains undeciphered.

The script is notable for its reverse boustrophedon pattern, where lines are read alternately from left to right and right to left, with the glyphs physically inverted in alternating lines. This distinctive pattern suggests a sophisticated understanding of writing principles, yet the meaning and purpose of the texts remain elusive.

The corpus of Rongorongo is extremely limited, consisting of only 26 known artifacts, some of which are damaged or fragmentary. This limited corpus presents a significant challenge to decipherment efforts, as traditional cryptographic and linguistic approaches typically require larger datasets to identify patterns reliably.



HISTORICAL CONTEXT
-----------------
Easter Island, located in the southeastern Pacific Ocean, was settled by Polynesian voyagers around 800-1200 CE. The island developed a unique culture, famous for its monumental stone statues (moai) and, later, the Rongorongo script.

The first European contact with Easter Island occurred in 1722 when Dutch explorer Jacob Roggeveen arrived on Easter Sunday (hence the island's name). By this time, the Rongorongo script was already in use among the island's elite. However, following European contact and subsequent Peruvian slave raids in the 1860s, the island's population was devastated, and much of the traditional knowledge, including the ability to read Rongorongo, was lost.

The first systematic collection of Rongorongo artifacts began in the 1870s. By this time, no living Rapanui could read or write the script, though some oral traditions about its use survived. These traditions suggest that Rongorongo was used for religious recitations, genealogical records, and possibly astronomical observations.

PREVIOUS DECIPHERMENT ATTEMPTS
-----------------------------
Numerous approaches have been attempted to decipher Rongorongo since its discovery. Major approaches include:

1. Logographic Hypothesis (Barthel, 1958)
   - Proposed that each glyph represents a word or concept
   - Identified potential calendar notations on Tablet Mamari
   - Limited success in identifying specific semantic domains

2. Genealogical Records Theory (Fischer, 1997)
   - Suggested tablets contain chiefly genealogies and creation myths
   - Proposed readings based on parallel oral traditions
   - Criticized for methodological issues and selective evidence

3. Rebus Principle Approach (Guy, 1990)
   - Proposed glyphs represent words that sound similar to other words
   - Similar to the principle used in Egyptian hieroglyphs
   - Limited success due to insufficient knowledge of ancient Rapanui phonology

4. Astronomical Calendar Theory (Wieczorek, 2016)
   - Identified potential astronomical observations in certain sequences
   - Focused on lunar and stellar cycles
   - Partial correlations with known Polynesian astronomical traditions

5. Statistical Pattern Analysis (Pozdniakov & Pozdniakov, 2007)
   - Applied computational linguistics methods to identify patterns
   - Discovered statistically significant bigrams and trigrams
   - Identified potential structural elements but no semantic breakthrough

6. Comparative Linguistic Approach (Horley, 2010)
   - Compared Rongorongo patterns with known Polynesian language structures
   - Identified potential grammatical markers
   - Limited by lack of bilingual texts for confirmation

None of these approaches has produced a generally accepted decipherment. The challenges include:
- Limited corpus size
- Lack of bilingual texts (no "Rosetta Stone")
- Discontinuity in cultural transmission
- Possible multifunctionality of the script (both semantic and mnemonic)
